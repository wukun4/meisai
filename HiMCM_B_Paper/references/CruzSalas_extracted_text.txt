Evaporative cooling                                          mist evaporative cooling systems (MECS). However, assessing their eﬀect using building thermal simulation
Mist                                                         programs requires a physical model of the MECS operating in combination with natural ventilation in semi-
Natural ventilation
                                                             outdoor environments (MECS&NV) that can be implemented in such programs. This research presents, step by
Semi-outdoor
Thermodynamic modeling
                                                             step, the development of a thermodynamic model for the MECS&NV process based on psychrometric theory,
Energyplus                                                   which can be implemented in EnergyPlus using its Python plug-in system. The developed model was validated
                                                             against experimental results obtained in a semi-outdoor cafeteria. For the cafeteria air temperature, the mean
                                                             absolute error is 0.4 ◦ C and the mean bias error is 0.1 ◦ C. The cooling eﬀect of the MECS on the cafeteria air
                                                             temperature compared with the value it would have without the MECS has a maximum reduction of 2.9 ◦ C and
                                                             an average of 1.9 ◦ C. The developed model can be used to perform future simulations and support decision-
                                                             making regarding sizing and implementation of this type of evaporative cooling system in semi-outdoor spaces
                                                             for low-energy buildings.

                                                                                                3.2.3. Instrumentation
                                                                                                    The air temperature in the cafeteria was measured employing a
                                                                                                2.90 m high tower with six thermocouples. It was placed near the cafe-
                                                                                                teria center (point Tc) in Fig. 6. The ﬁrst thermocouple was at the top,
                                                                                                and the rest were 0.30 m apart on a vertical line. Temperature data were
                                                                                                recorded through a data acquisition system with open hardware and
                                                                                                software. Relative humidity was measured at the center of the cafeteria
                                                                                                (point Tc) with a Fluke meter with its acquisition system. In addition,
                                                                                                a three-component ultrasound anemometer was used, with its center at
                                                                                                a height of 1.45 m, the middle eﬀective height of the cafeteria (point
                                                                                                Ac) in Fig. 6. To measure outdoor air velocity, a similar anemometer
                                                                                                was placed in the square (point As), also shown in Fig. 6, aligned with
                                                                                                the ﬁrst and 8.85 m from the cafeteria entrance. These anemometers
                                                                                                were connected to computers for data acquisition. The velocity of the
                                                                                                outdoor air on the side of the lattice wall was impossible to measure due
                                                                                                to the sloping terrain. Table 1 summarizes the equipment used and the
                                                                                                associated measurement uncertainties for each experimental variable.
